# Nothing to Something

In this, and two further articles, I want to tell you the story of
how a growing IoT business realized the value of metrics, and built
telemetry found into the heart of everything.

I will tell you why we did things, how we did them, what we got
right, what we got wrong, and try to explain how important Wavefront
has become to everything we do.

## What's the Problem?

Our industry is full of solutions in search of problems. A
monitoring system like Wavefront looks expensive and complicated.
Your business may be doing just fine without much telemetry, so why
would you bother? I'll tell you why we did.

In 2015 I was working as an operations engineer for a startup
running entirely in AWS.  Things had grown organically -- and
chaotically -- to a point where we had little to no idea what
anything was doing, or even what we had. We experimented with an
early version of a proprietary SaaS telemetry system, and our
experiments were unsuccessful. We tried cobbling something together
ourselves, and that was unsuccessful too.

Things were failing silently. We assumed we had dozens, if not
hundreds, of EC2 instances doing virtually nothing but costing us
something. We were scared
to change anything because we couldn't tell what impacts a change
might have. We had no confidence in our systems. And that's a bad
feeling.

Something had to be done, so the central operations-cum-tooling team
was tasked with encouraging  and helping the product teams adopt
certain industry-standard tools. One of these was Nagios. Nagios and
its ilk, if you don't know, run small scripts on all your hosts. The
exit codes of those scripts are interpreted, broadly, as "OK" or
"not OK", and operators are notified accordingly. A properly
designed Nagios system should give you some
confidence that your system is functioning within a known set of
parameters. A classic script might exit `0` (OK) if CPU usage is
lower than 80%, and `1` (not OK) if it's not.

No one really *likes* Nagios. But, it's better than nothing, and
nothing is exactly what we had. So, we started rolling Nagios out to
the product teams.

As I said, Nagios tells you that your predfined thresholds are not
being breached. At least, that they're not being breached *at the
time the checking scripts run*. What happens in between -- who
knows? And how do you *set* those thresholds? In other words, how do
you know what normal looks like?

Software like Cacti, Munin, and the dominant Graphite attempted to
answer that question. These systems collect and collate time-series
metrics; generally performance-related things like load average and
disk space, and put them into charts. They usually allow the user to
manipulate and correlate the data.

In a previous job I had seen Graphite done right. Every important
metric was collected, piped across physical networks into a big cluster
through a fiendishly complicated home-rolled forwarding system, then
analysed in a meaningful way. It was reliable, and it provided
enormous value for monitoring, tuning, and debugging. In my next
job, I saw Graphite done wrong. That company had a simpler network and
a far simpler system, yet there, Graphite almost never worked. I spent
the majority of my time fighting it, scaling it up, adding storage,
adding processor power, and every time I thought I had it, it broke
again, and it broke my spirit.

The difference was that the first site was static. Operating in a
physical datacentre, in a strongly regulated industry with heavy
change control and a great deal of process, the things they
measured didn't much change, and Graphite had been scaled and tuned
until it was just right. The second site was a startup, in AWS,
scaling up and down, building and tearing down instances and
environments every day, adding new features regularly, and
constantly instrumenting more and more parts of a distributed system
to understand it better.  Graphite can't cope with that. Can't cope
with change. Most of us can't.

Our situation was far more like the second site, and I very
much did not relish the thought of wrangling Graphite again. Or
Nagios, another product designed for the old days of static estates,
which I knew from experience, required some heavy marshalling to
make it work in the cloud.

Then, we hired an engineer who I had worked with on my second
Graphite job. "Graphite's gone", he told me. "We got this other
thing, and it's brilliant". Now, when a grizzled seen-it-all ops
guy tells you something's brilliant, you have to take notice.

I'd heard about this thing -- *Wavefront* -- from him before, but it
didn't even have a website at that point, so I didn't know
much about it at all. He showed me their demo system and I thought, "yeah,
it's prettier Graphite. What's so good about it?".

The thing that was most immediately obviously good about Wavefront
was that it was a service, run off-site, by someone else.  As the
poor sap who'd tried (and failed) to keep Graphite afloat, I
appreciated the value of that, and it fitted with the way
we were trying to do business: concentrating our efforts on our
products, paying other people to do the things which didn't
differntiate us in the market. This not only lets your
engineers concentrate on what's important, but those people running
the telemetry, or the CI pipeline, or the source control: that's
*all* they do. They're really good at it, and whether you believe it
or not, their solution *will* be better than the one you build
yourself from bits off Github.

The second thing was that, Wavefront, apparently, always worked,
however much data the devs threw at it. It didn't run out of disk
space because someone changed the granularity of the DB profiling,
and it didn't slump to its knees every time there was an outage and
three engineers tried to load a big dashboard to debug it.

The next thing I was told about took me marginally longer to grasp.
That was the value of alerting off telemetry.

If we record everything a system is doing, and we have a good
graphing engine, we can *see* what its normal behaviour looks like.
We can draw thresholds on axes and say "if this dips below that,
something ain't right". If you use Nagios, how often have you logged
on to a box to investigate an alert to find that the alerting
condition has gone away? Alerting off telemetry, you can ask only to
be notified if the moving average of a metric exceeds a threshold for
twenty minutes. That's less likely to fire false positives.
You can also build composite alerts from multiple, unrelated
sources. There's infinite scope to be clever. The days of alerting
off load average have gone.

## What do I Do?

Do what we did!  We spoke to Wavefront, and got ourselves an account
on their shared cluster. (There's a [free
trial](https://www.wavefront.com/sign-up/) now, so things are much
easier.) We got an endpoint,
and a login to the UI. With that login the first engineer can invite
others, and generate tokens for proxies.

Proxies? Right: Wavefront differs from most other SaaS monitoring
products, in that it does not force you to install a proprietary
agent on your hosts. Instead, you set up a proxy, then run something
-- anything -- on your hosts which can send metrics to the proxies.
There's a [well documented wire
format](https://docs.wavefront.com/wavefront_data_format.html), or
you can use Graphite or OpenTSDB format, which many things already
speak. (An increasing amount of software now speaks native
Wavefront, but it didn't when we started our journey.)

The proxies are all about ingesting data easily and quickly. So,
inbound traffic is unencrypted, and unauthenticated.  You therefore
must be careful where you put the proxies and how you configure your
network. As we were running in AWS, we put our proxies in a security
group which only accepts traffic from the `WFSG` security group in
the same VPC. If a host doesn't have `WFSG`, it ain't sending
metrics.  My own company runs in the Joyent public cloud, so the
proxy has a cloud firewall rule which only accepts traffic on 2878
from "all my VMs".  In my lab setup my proxy is wide open, but it's
on a private VLAN.  Different environments have different ways of
restricting proxy ingress, but there's nothing Wavefront-specific.
Nothing new to learn or to get wrong, just the application of
existing knowledge. I like this approach very much.

We didn't build just one proxy of course, but set up a pair behind a
load balancer. A simple old-skool ELB is good enough, with the proxy
hosts in an autoscaling group so that should one die or go out of
service, it will be automatically replaced. We are a Puppet shop, so
we put together a simple module to configure the proxies. We
also wrote Cloudformation to launch all of this, and wrapped a
script around it so the product teams (our customers) could stand
up their own proxies with minimal effort. Pretty simple so far,
right?

## What do I call Things?

At this point we had to think about what to call our metrics. Metrics
are dot-separated strings, with each part being significant. The
convention is that each segment of the metric is more specific than
the one which preceeded it.

Our principal challenge was that we had to cater for multiple
product teams, each with multiple environments. The business
higher-ups wanted to be able to make estate-wide queries, so we had
to enforce some common pattern.

A further problem was that we were not sure we would be
allowed to keep Wavefront. It was a largely unknown product, and we
had trouble getting a closed-beta startup with no website through
corporate procurement!  Because of this we had to design in a
downward-compatible way, and downwards for us, in 2015, was
Graphite. In Graphite, *all* your metric information must be encoded
in the path. So, we ended up with a path like this:

```
product.environment.role.host.metric
```


We use the `product:environment:role` triumvirate everywhere. We
call it an "identifier", so all our engineers were already familiar
with it. The final `metric` part of the path is decided by whatever
is generating the metrics, more of which later. Graphite actually
sneaks in the hostname, but it's stripped out of the metric path by
the server (or in this case the Wavefront proxy).

Initiall we had problems with people trying to send their own
metrics, or messing about with the collectd config, and trashing our
nice clean metric namespace. When Wavefront introduced proxy
whitelisting, we set up rules so that proxies would reject any
metric which didn't conform to our standards. People complained at
first, but they thanked us later.

The proxies have many internal metrics, giving you superb
visibility. The rate of rejected metrics is easy to view, and proxy
logs will tell you what and why.


If I were doing this work again, a metric like

```
ops.prod.fluentd-server.host.cpu0.percent-idle
```

would be rephrased as

```
cpu0.percent-idle
```

This shorter version perhaps looks useless. If everything
reports CPU idle time in that way, how can you ever tell what it is?
You use *point tags*. These are key-value pairs attached to each
data point which describe it in any number of ways. Remember I said
the proxy stripped the hostname out of the metric path? It turns
that into a `source` tag. You can add as many tags as you like, so,
that second example might really, under the surface, look like this:

```
cpu0.percent-idle source=i-12345678 product=ops environment=prod
role=fluentd-server region=eu-west-1 instanceType=m3.large
```

With the benefit of experience, I would strongly encourage you to
use the tagged approach. Filtering on tags is far more flexible than
using wildcards in the metric path, and complex queries are easier
to read and write.


## What are My Hosts Doing?

At the point we started using Wavefront, we ran pretty much everything
in EC2. That is, on virtual machines which behave as if they real
computers. We therefore, obviously, wanted to collect host metrics.
These are useful in any environment to spot performance bottlenecks,
but become doubly useful in the cloud, where "right-sizing"
instances can be extremely important.

The most venerable host metric collector is probably
[collectd](https://collectd.org/). This is written in C, and out of
the box, will collect a good spread of performance metrics on pretty
much any operating system you can think of, and perhaps a couple you
can't. It's been around long enough that you can trust it, and its
choice of language mean it's lightweight, and has a small footprint.

As well as the standard OS stuff, like CPU/memory/disk, there are
collectd plugins for lots of applications and non-standard corners of the
OS. If you're using Postgres, or RabbitMQ, or ZFS, there are plugins
you can build in to collectd that will give you useful information.
You can also write your own plugins, and though the preferred method
is C, there are APIs which let you use Perl and Python.

We felt the onus was to give the product teams something stable and
reliable, with the lowest possible barrier to entry.  Because it was
so well-established, and because it provided a Graphite format
output plugin which would talk to Wavefront with no persuading, we
chose collectd.

We built our own binaries to get a reconnect feature which hadn't
yet hit an official released, and packaged them up. Then we wrote a
Puppet module which installed the software and configured it to use
the metric namespace pattern we had settled on. Anyone who took than
Puppet module automatically got host metrics, and if they moved
between teams, they'd find their new team's metrics in exactly the
same place and format as they were used to.

Though collectd was fine for host metrics, we wanted to make it easy
for product developers to generate application metrics.  Expecting
them to write a collectd plugin isn't reasonable, so we had to find
something else, more geared to *receiving* than *fetching* metrics.

Developers, for some reason, love
[statsd](https://github.com/etsy/statsd). Statsd listens for
metrics, and rolls them up over a predefined interval, condensing
very many data points into averages, percentiles and the like. It
makes high-volume metrics manageable, and it's *very* easy to talk
to. Every language has a dozen statsd modules, even though the wire
format is incredibly simple.  So we packaged that up too.  (We had
to: the default package started the daemon with a default config on
install, which failed, and broke our Puppet runs.) Then we extended
our Puppet module to configure statsd to listen for metrics from the
local host, and send them on in Graphite format, tailored for our
namespace. We chose to put collectd metrics under a `host` path, and
statsd under `app`. So now, whatever box you were on, if you sent
application metrics to `localhost:8912`, they magically ended up in
Wavefront too. We couldn't have made it much simpler.

Our strategy has served us reasonably well.  Collectd and statsd are
a bit "old-world", and perhaps don't deal with transience,
particularly disappearing endpoints, quite as well as they might.
Developing plugins is a little clunky too, but the real problem is a
lack of tagging support. We get some point tagging via proxy
"preprocessor rules", which look at an incoming metric and apply
tags to it based on rules.

For instance, the following chunk of YAML tells the proxy to pull
the product, environment, and role out of a Graphite-format metric,
and add them all as point tags.

```yaml
'2003':
  - rule: product
    action: extractTag
    tag: product
    source: metricName
    search: "^([^\\.]*)\\.([^\\.]*)\\.([^\\.]*)\\..*"
    replace: "$1"
  - rule: environment
    action: extractTag
    tag: environment
    source: metricName
    search: "^([^\\.]*)\\.([^\\.]*)\\.([^\\.]*)\\..*"
    replace: "$2"
  - rule: role
    action: extractTag
    tag: role
    source: metricName
    search: "^([^\\.]*)\\.([^\\.]*)\\.([^\\.]*)\\..*"
    replace: "$3"
```

Preprocessor rules also let you apply "static" tags. For instance, a
proxy in eu-west-1 could tag every point with `region=eu-west-1`.

In my other work (the stuff I mentioned earlier, running in Joyent's
cloud), I chose [Diamond](https://github.com/python-diamond/Diamond)
rather than collectd. Diamond is written entirely in Python, with a
reasonably nice API, which makes it easier and more pleasant to
extend than collectd. It is in active development (at the time of
writing) and it natively supports tagging. Some time ago I wrote [a
handler (output plugin) which speaks the native Wavefront wire
format](https://github.com/python-diamond/Diamond), and submitted it
as a PR, but it hasn't been merged yet. I've been running it in
production for over a year with no issues though, so it's pretty
well proven.

Diamond uses a bit more memory than collectd, but I've had good
experiences with it, and definitely prefer it to the older system.
It does, of course, require a whole Python installation to make it
work, but most OSes have one of those anyway.

The most modern (hipster?) metric collector is
[Telegraf](https://www.influxdata.com/time-series-platform/telegraf/).
Written in Go, it has support for a wide range of applications, a
very clean API, and is super-easy to deploy as it's a single binary
and one config file. Telegraf has [a Wavefront
output](https://github.com/wavefrontHQ/telegraf), supports tags, and
goes further than collectd and Diamond in the way it gathers
metrics. As well as going out and scraping `/proc` or whatever, it
will run listeners, speak RESTfully to endpoints, and all manner of
other things. No way of collecting numbers is off-limits to
Telegraf. It's a very nice piece of software, and shoud, in my
opinion, be the default choice for anyone collecting metrics of any
kind today. Telegraf is easy to extend, so long as you don't mind
building from source, and I've had fun writing [some SmartOS
specific](https://github.com/snltd/solaris-telegraf-plugins)
plugins as a way of learning Go.

We are currently looking at migrating from collectd and statsd to
Telgraf, almost solely because our product teams want to be able to
tag. There are numerous forks of statsd which support tagging, but
Telegraf has such flexibility and power that we feel it would
empower our users, and that's what we want.

These days we have ways of interrogating hosts beyond reading
`/proc` or parsing CLI output. Dynamic tracing is becoming
increasingly popular (unless you come from the Solaris world, where
it's been popular for a dozen or so years), and it's not at all
difficult to feed DTrace, Sysdig, eBPF, or any other, numbers into
Wavefront. Here's a quick one-liner (albeit a slightly *long* line)
which will show us which processes are making system calls:

```cmd
# dtrace -n 'syscall:::entry { @num[execname] = count(); }
             profile:::tick-1sec { printa(@num) }' \
  | grep "[0-9]$" | wf write file -m dtrace.syscalls -F mv     -
```

Wavefront will, with no tuning or intervention on your part, accept
an insane amount of metrics, so it's tempting to just throw everything
at it. But, you'll be charged for the rate of metrics you put in,
and it's amazing how quickly they can rack up.

So, what do you *really* need to know about your hosts? One of the
first things we disabled was the collectd `vmem` collector. On every
collection it generates about 70 metrics per host. A thousand
instances, with a 10 second granularity, and you're doing 7000
points per second just to know how many transparent anonymous
hugepages and reclaimable slabs you have. They probably aren't
the business's core KPIs.

CPU metrics can get expensive too. By default collectd on Ubuntu
gives you eight CPU metrics per core. Do you *really* need to be
able break down `softirq` usage per core? Because if you don't, and
you've got a fleet of Xeon E5 boxes, you're going to be generating
*a lot* of useless information. Not only are you paying for this
junk, but you might end up having to filter it out of alerts and
queries, or it might be slowing down useful calculations, and you
might have to scale up your proxies to accommodate it. Do you
even need *anything* per-CPU? Most CPU collectors will aggregate CPU
usage across all cores, and if you only want to know whether the box
is generally over- or under-used, that might well be enough.
Remember you have the power to run different collectors at different
intervals. You might feel the need to measure CPU utilisation every
ten seconds, but do you need to check the amount of free inodes in
`/var` as often as that?

As our engineers and developers came to appreciate the power of
Wavefront, we noticed big jumps in our point rate, and we tried to
reign it back in by dropping unused host metrics. Most collectors
have fine-grained control of what they send (collectd is the weakest
in this regard), but the proxies have the ability to white- and
black-list metrics, so you have plenty of control.

Think also about the rate at which your collectors run. Your
workload may mean that it's useful to see how hot your CPUs are
every few seconds,

One approach I've used is to run more detailed, higher resolution,
metrics on one host of a tier. This keeps the point rate down, but
gives you the introspection to debug issues, or be sure that a
release of OS upgrade hasn't affected anything too much.
