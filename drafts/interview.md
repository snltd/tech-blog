Everyhwere I work, I end up telling this story. So I might as well
write it down.

About fifteen years ago, I was being interviewed for some sys-admin
job or other. There were two people, my prospective manager, and
some techie. I remember nothing of the manager's questions, so they
must have been pretty standard. Then over to the techie, who asked
me, with some gravitas, the single most stupid question I've ever
been asked at interview.

```quote
Four letters of the alphabet are not valid options in GNU `ls`. What
are they?
```

The story kind of breaks down here, because it's so long ago, I
can't remember what I said. If it had been the late '90s, in my 20s,
head overinflated from picking and choosing ridiculously remunerated
dotcom jobs, I'd have said something smart-arsed, dropped the mic,
and made myself a better story. But I think it was around 2003-2004
time, when being able to build clustered E10ks commanded £17.50 an
hour.  (Really. I was offered that once.) So I suspect I said
something like "I don't know, but `--help` will tell you. I regret
that now.

I don't remember whether I was offered the job, but I certain;y
didn't end up doing it. I don't think I want to be subordinate to
someone who values knowledge like that.

Another time, similar situation, for a Solaris admin job, the tech
asked me "what patch level" my current estate ran on. I'd never
heard that term outside of a Windows service pack context, so asked
him to explain what he meant. He repeated the question. I asked
again. He repeated the question. I exchanged looks with the manager.
Eventually he said "when you type `uname -a.`..." and I twigged: he
meant which kernel patch was installed. I explained that we had
almost a thousand workstations, and five (maybe six?) staging
environments all on different OS revisions, along with various
production envs, some locked down very tightly with "never change
this" type instructions (It was aerospace manufacturing, which is
conservative to say the least.) So, in short, I didn't know. He
smugly replied "I know what mine are" and wrote something in his
pad. The manager gave me a sympathetic eye-roll. I figured they must
have a pretty easy life, polishing the silver to that degree, so
took the job. The week before I was going to start, they moved the
whole project to India. It failed, and they offered it me again six
months later.
